Install Plumbing for a Tub: What Is Involved

A tub needs a drain, a trap, supply lines, and an overflow. Here is how the work is staged and why it is not a casual weekend job.

By the Helena Express Plumbing team. Published October 15, 2026. Updated October 15, 2026.

Installing plumbing for a tub means running a drain with a trap, connecting hot and cold supply lines to a valve, and building an overflow, all before the tub is set and the walls are closed. The work happens in stages, and the order matters because most of it ends up hidden behind a finished surface. A mistake in the drain or valve is expensive to reach later.

This post walks through the stages in plain terms. It describes what the work involves and why a licensed plumber usually does it. For rules in your area, ask the Helena building department.

The big picture in four stages

Tub plumbing follows a predictable sequence. First comes planning and layout, then rough-in, then testing, and finally setting the tub and finishing the connections. Each stage ends with a check before the next begins.

Knowing the order helps you schedule other trades. Framing, plumbing, electrical, drywall, and tile all depend on the plumbing being right the first time.

Stage one: layout and planning

Layout decisions before any pipe is cut

  1. Choose the tub type, since freestanding, alcove, and drop-in styles all need different drain and valve locations.
  2. Get the manufacturer's specification sheet for the exact drain position.
  3. Mark the drain and valve centerlines on the floor and wall framing.
  4. Check floor joists and the subfloor for the path of the drain.
  5. Decide where the access panel will go if the tub has a hidden trap or valve.

Model codes such as the International Plumbing Code (IPC) and the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) are published for towns and counties to adopt, often with local changes. Which rules apply at your address depends on what the local building department has adopted, so ask them before work starts.

The spec sheet is the most overlooked item. Drain location varies between brands, and moving a drain after the floor is closed can mean cutting out finished work.

Stage two: the rough-in

Rough-in is the pipe work installed before the walls and floor are closed. For a tub, it includes the drain line, the trap, the vent connection, and the supply lines that run to the valve body.

A drain needs to slope toward the main line so water leaves the tub, and a trap holds water to block sewer gas. The vent lets air in behind the water so the tub empties without gurgling. Each of these has to be sized and placed so the tub drains quickly and quietly.

Stage three: test before closing the walls

Before anyone covers the pipes, the plumber tests them. Drain lines are filled with water or put under test pressure to look for leaks. Supply lines are pressurized with water or air and watched for a drop.

Tests like this are common in inspections. In some places a plumber must be present for inspection stages. Ask the Helena building department what it requires for a tub install in your address, since we cannot state those rules for you.

Stage four: setting the tub and finishing

Once the rough-in passes, the tub is set, often into a bed of mortar or on a manufacturer supported base so it does not flex. Then the drain and overflow are connected, the valve trim is installed, and the surround or tile goes up.

This is also when the final leak check is done. Run water, fill the tub, and let it drain while someone watches the trap and every joint.

Why hire a plumber for this

A tub drain that leaks inside a wall or floor can run for weeks before showing up as a stain or soft spot. By then the subfloor, insulation, and ceiling below can be wet. The cost of fixing hidden water damage is far higher than the cost of installing the drain right.

If you hire someone, you can check a plumber's license through the Alabama Plumbers and Gas Fitters Examining Board's public lookup. A clear written scope and price before work starts protects both sides.

Pricing factors

What shapes the price of tub plumbing

  • Tub typeFreestanding tubs need floor-mounted supply and a drain that lines up exactly. Alcove tubs are simpler.
  • Floor and framingConcrete slabs need cutting. Wood floors need joist checks and sometimes notching or blocking.
  • Distance to main drainA short run to an existing drain costs less than a long new one.
  • Valve and trimPressure-balanced and thermostatic valves cost more than basic ones.
  • AccessA bathroom on a second floor or over a finished ceiling takes more labor.

Insurance usually pays for sudden water damage, not for new fixture installation. Ask your agent whether work on a drain affects your coverage.

Checklist before work begins

Get ready for the plumber

  • Have the tub and its specification sheet on site before rough-in.
  • Clear the bathroom and the area under it if there is a crawlspace or basement.
  • Find your main water shutoff so it can be closed if needed.
  • Confirm with the building department what inspection is required.
  • Decide on valve and trim, since the valve body goes in during rough-in.
  • Plan where an access panel will go.

Safety checklist before a tub project starts

  • Shut off the water supply and open a faucet to relieve pressure
  • Switch off power to anything near the work area
  • Confirm the floor and subfloor are dry and solid before cutting
  • Call a plumber before opening walls near gas or electrical lines
