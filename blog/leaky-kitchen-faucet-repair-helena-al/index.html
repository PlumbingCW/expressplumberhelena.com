Leaky Kitchen Faucet Repair in Helena, AL

Where the water shows up tells you what failed. Spout, handle, base or the pipes below the sink each point to a different part.

By the Helena Express Plumbing team. Published October 15, 2026. Updated October 15, 2026.

A kitchen faucet leaks from one of four places: the spout, the handle, the base, or the supply lines and valves underneath. Each spot points to a different worn part. A drip from the spout usually means a worn cartridge, washer or seat. Water around the handle means worn seals. Water pooling around the base or inside the cabinet means a failed seal, a loose connection or a split supply line.

Start by watching where the water first appears, then dry everything with a towel and check again. That one test tells you whether you have a ten-minute fix or a job for a plumber, and it tells you whether the water is reaching your cabinet floor.

Match the leak to the cause

Use the table below as a first guide. It is not a diagnosis for every faucet brand, but it covers the common patterns.

Where the faucet leaks and what usually causes it
Where you see waterLikely causeTypical fix
Drip from the spout tip, constantWorn cartridge, washer or valve seatReplace the cartridge or seat parts
Drip only when the handle is onWorn internal sealReplace the cartridge or O-rings
Water around the handle baseWorn O-ring or loose packing nutReplace seals and tighten the nut
Water at the faucet base on the counterFailed base seal or loose mountingReplace the seal or tighten the nut from below
Wet cabinet floor under the sinkLoose or split supply line, shutoff valve leakReplace the line or valve
Weak flow plus a leak at the spout swivelWorn spout O-ringsReplace the O-rings

Stop the water first

Do this before you start guessing at parts. A small drip can ruin a cabinet floor if it runs for a few weeks, and a failed supply line can drain a lot of water in a short time.

Quick steps when a kitchen faucet starts leaking

  1. Close the two shutoff valves under the sink by turning them clockwise. If they will not move, close the main water valve for the house.
  2. Open the faucet to drain the lines and confirm the water is off.
  3. Dry the faucet and the cabinet, then place a towel in the cabinet floor.
  4. Turn the water back on briefly and watch where the first drop forms.
  5. Take a photo of the faucet brand and model, which is often on the base or under the sink.
  6. Decide whether to repair, replace or call, using the sections below.

Spout drips: the cartridge or seat

The most common kitchen faucet drip comes from the cartridge in a single-handle faucet, or a washer and seat in an older two-handle one. These parts wear from years of turning and from mineral deposits in the water.

Cartridges are model-specific, so you need the faucet's brand and model before you buy one. A wrong cartridge may fit but still leak. If your faucet has no visible brand, a plumber can usually identify it by removing the handle and cap.

Handle and base leaks

Water around the handle means the seals inside the handle body are worn or the packing nut is loose. This is often a simple repair, but corroded fittings can make it harder than it looks, and forcing a stuck part can crack the faucet body.

A leak at the base is more serious. It often means the mounting is loose, the seal has failed, or water is running down the inside of the faucet body into the countertop hole. If the counter is stone or laminate, repeated wetting can damage the edge. Shut the water and look underneath for staining.

Under the sink: supply lines and shutoff valves

If the cabinet floor is wet, check the supply lines first. Flexible braided lines can fail at the fittings or split inside the braid with no outside warning. Shutoff valves can also seep at the stem or the connection, particularly when they have not been turned in years.

Replace a leaking supply line instead of tightening it over and over, and test shutoff valves by turning them gently. If a valve will not close or starts to leak when you touch it, a plumber should replace it so you have a working shutoff. A faucet job often goes faster and safer when the valves under the sink work properly.

Repair or replace

Repairing is the right call for a newer faucet with a worn cartridge. Replacing is better when the faucet is old, corroded, pitted or has had a repair before. Some faucets are cheap enough that repair parts and labor approach the price of a new fixture. Others are high-end and worth fixing.

Ask your plumber about the faucet's age, parts availability and finish condition. Also consider whether you are planning a countertop change or a sink swap soon, since replacing the faucet at the same time saves a visit.

For a straightforward fix or a swap, our faucet repair team can check the parts and give a clear price before work starts. If the leak comes from a pipe behind the wall rather than the faucet, leak detection is the right service.

What to check before calling a plumber

  • Is the water shut off at the sink valves, and did it stop?
  • Where does the first drop of water appear?
  • Is the cabinet floor wet, soft or stained?
  • Can you find the faucet brand and model?
  • Does the leak happen only when the faucet is on, or all the time?
  • Is the faucet loose on the counter when you press it?
  • Have you noticed a higher water bill?

Helena homes and kitchen faucets

A large share of Helena homes outside Old Town were built after 2000, since the city grew from 10,296 people in 2000 to 20,914 in 2020. Original kitchen faucets in those homes are often builder-grade, and that means a worn cartridge is common once the fixture is a decade or two old.

Replacing a builder-grade faucet with a better one is often a simple upgrade, and the tie to a repair is clear: if the part is cheap and the faucet is solid, repair. If both are low quality, replace.

In Old Town, expect older shutoff valves and more corrosion at the fittings. That is where a replacement of the valves during the faucet job pays off.

Water use and leaks

EPA WaterSense reports that the average household wastes about 180 gallons of water per week, roughly 9,400 gallons per year, from leaks. A faucet that drips all day is part of that. The same agency says the average American uses about 82 gallons of water per day at home, so a steady drip adds up faster than people expect.

In Helena, water and sewer are billed through the City of Helena Utilities Board, reachable at (205) 663-1670. If your bill rises with no change in how you use water, a leaking faucet or supply line is a cheap thing to rule out.

When to call

Call a plumber if water is running into the cabinet, if the shutoff valves will not close, if the faucet is corroded, or if you are not sure where the water comes from.
