What the service line is, and who owns which part

The service line runs underground from the water meter to the shutoff valve at your house. It carries every drop of water you use, so a break stops everything at once and a slow leak quietly wastes water and softens the ground around it.

Where the utility's responsibility ends and yours begins varies by system and by address. Do not assume. Ask the City of Helena Utilities Board, at (205) 663-1670, which section is yours before you spend money on a repair. For a leak that sits between the meter and the house, that answer usually decides who calls a plumber.

Signs of a failing line

Not every sign is dramatic. Some are subtle for weeks before a clear failure. Watch for these:

  • A green, soggy or sunken patch in the yard when it has not rained.
  • Water bubbling up in a driveway, walk or flower bed.
  • A meter that keeps moving when every fixture is off.
  • A bill that climbs without a change in habits.
  • Pressure that drops in the whole house, not just one fixture.
  • Muddy or discolored water, or air spitting from taps after the water has been off.
  • The sound of running water outdoors or near the meter box.

If the ground around the line is soft and sinking, keep kids and pets off it. Water can wash soil away from under a driveway or walkway before the surface shows any damage.

When the water stops or pressure drops: a night-time emergency

Total loss of water has several causes. The utility may be doing work or have an outage, a valve may be closed, a pipe inside the house may have failed, or the service line may have broken. Before calling, check whether neighbors have water, look at the meter for movement, and confirm the main valve at the house is fully open.

If only your house is affected and the meter shows no movement with a faucet open, call us. The break could be anywhere along the line. If the meter spins but you have no water inside, the loss may be at a break before it reaches the house, and that is an emergency worth a night call.

Low pressure is slower and more confusing. A partly clogged pressure regulator, a partly closed valve, a corroded section or a small leak can all reduce pressure. We check the incoming pressure with a gauge to see whether the problem is in the line, the regulator or the plumbing inside. If the whole street has weak pressure, the Helena Utilities Board is the first call.

Finding the leak before anyone digs

Digging blindly turns a small repair into a yard-wide project. We use the meter, pressure testing and listening equipment to narrow down the failure, then confirm the spot before opening the ground. If the line is plastic, sound travels poorly, so pressure testing and other methods carry more of the work.

Before any excavation, call 811 so buried gas, electric and communications lines are marked. Mention your plan to the utility as well, because some repairs near the meter need its involvement. If your property has irrigation, mark the zone valves and tell us where the lines run.

Repair or replace

Not every failed service line needs to be replaced. The choice depends on the age and material of the pipe, how many failures it has had, the depth, and what is above it. Here are the options we usually discuss:

How we usually decide

  1. Locate the leak and confirm it is on your side of the meter.
  2. Check the pipe material, depth and condition around the break.
  3. If the pipe is sound apart from one spot, repair only the damaged section.
  4. If the pipe is corroded, brittle or has multiple failures, price a full replacement from the meter to the house.
  5. Choose open trench or a trenchless method based on what is above the line, such as driveway, trees or landscaping.
  6. Test, backfill and restore, then confirm the meter is still after we finish.

Open trench is the most direct way to replace a line and often the most affordable. Trenchless methods, which pull or burst a new pipe through the old path, disturb less landscaping but suit only some situations. We will describe both, along with what each one leaves behind, so you can choose before work begins.

Why Helena properties are a mixed picture

Helena went from 3,918 residents in 1990 to 20,914 in 2020, and its housing stock reflects that. Growth areas such as Dearing Downs and Old Cahaba are newer than Old Town, but a younger house does not guarantee a trouble-free line. Old Town may have older lines, and a recurring wet spot or falling pressure there deserves a look sooner rather than later.

Lines also pass through varied ground. Roots, shifting soil, and rock can all stress a buried pipe, and heavy equipment above it can crush a shallow one. If you are planning a driveway, fence or pool, tell us where the line runs so the plan does not put new load on it.

What affects the cost of service line repair

  • Length and depthA short, shallow section costs less to reach than a long run or one deep under pavement.
  • What is above the lineConcrete, a driveway, trees and landscaping each change how the line can be reached and restored.
  • Repair or full replacementA spot repair is limited to one section, while a full replacement covers the whole run to the meter.
  • Pipe material and fittingsThe pipe type, pressure regulator and connections chosen affect parts and labor.
  • Permits and utility involvementSome work requires coordination with the utility or the building department, so check with the Helena building department or the utility.

Homeowners' insurance often does not cover the buried line itself, but some policies and add-on riders do, so ask your insurer. You will see a clear price before work starts, and we explain what is included.

Water you cannot afford to waste

Buried leaks waste water without any visible sign. The EPA's WaterSense program estimates that the average household loses about 180 gallons per week to leaks, or around 9,400 gallons per year. A service line leak can exceed that, since it runs under pressure around the clock. A bigger bill is often the first hint.

If the meter moves with every tap off, that is worth chasing even when the yard looks dry. For finding the exact spot, see leak detection. If the failed line is older, repiping may be part of the conversation inside the house too.

Before the plumber arrives

  • Close the main valve at the house, and confirm whether the meter stops.
  • Clear a path to the meter box and the main valve.
  • Note where you have seen wet ground, and take photos.
  • Mark the position of irrigation lines, sprinkler heads and any buried items you know about.
  • Call 811 if digging may be needed, or let us know and we will advise.
  • Move vehicles from the driveway if the line runs beneath it.
  • Have your utility account number ready in case the utility needs to be contacted.
