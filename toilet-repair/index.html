Overflow, leak, or constant running: which one do you have?

Most toilet calls fall into three groups. The bowl is clogged and rising, water is leaking from the tank or the base, or the tank refills over and over without anyone touching it. Each has a different cause and a different fix, so the first job is naming the symptom.

Toilet symptoms, likely causes, and the first move

A toilet problem that cannot wait until morning

A toilet leak is not always a small problem. Water at the base can soak the subfloor, and a ceiling below a second-floor bathroom can stain or sag within hours. If sewage is on the floor, treat it as contaminated and keep children and pets out of the room.

Call right away if the bowl overflows with the valve shut, if water is reaching a ceiling below, or if both toilets in the house will not flush. That last pattern usually points to the main line instead of one fixture. Our drain cleaning crew handles that side of it.

Never touch a light switch or outlet while standing in water. If water has reached an outlet or a power strip, shut off that circuit at the panel only if you can reach it dry, or leave the room and call 911 if you feel unsafe.

What we look at when we arrive

We start by watching the toilet work. A flush test shows whether the bowl drains clean, whether the tank refills properly, and whether anything weeps at the base or the supply line. Then we pull what is needed to fix the cause instead of the symptom.

For a rocking toilet or a damp floor, that usually means lifting the bowl, checking the flange, and setting a new wax ring or seal. For a running toilet, it means the flapper, fill valve, or flush valve inside the tank. For a bowl that will not clear, it means augering the trap and testing the line past it.

Repair or replace the toilet

Not every toilet is worth saving. A cracked bowl or tank cannot be sealed, and a toilet that has needed repeat visits may cost more to keep fixing than to swap. Older units that use far more water per flush can also be worth retiring when a repair is already on the table.

  • Repair: a worn flapper, fill valve, supply line, or wax ring on a sound bowl.
  • Replace: a cracked porcelain, a loose or damaged flange that keeps failing, or a model with parts nobody stocks.
  • Look at the flange first: a toilet that keeps rocking after a new seal often has a floor or flange problem, not a bad toilet.

If you are leaning toward a swap, tell us on the call. We can talk through new installation and removal of the old toilet before we arrive.

Before the plumber arrives

  • Close the supply valve behind the toilet (clockwise).
  • Stop flushing and keep everyone out of the bathroom if water is on the floor.
  • Mop standing water with towels and open a window or run a fan.
  • Move rugs, bath mats, and anything valuable away from the wet area.
  • Take a photo of any damage for your insurance claim.
  • Keep a bucket nearby in case the tank or line drips while you wait.

What affects the price of a toilet repair

  • Cause of the problemA new flapper or fill valve is a small job. A damaged flange or a hidden drain blockage is a bigger one.
  • Parts and the toilet modelCommon parts are quick to source. Older or specialty models can take longer.
  • Floor and flange conditionWater-damaged subfloor or a corroded flange adds repair steps before the toilet can be reset.
  • Repair or full replacementReplacing a toilet adds the new unit, haul-away of the old one, and setting time.
  • Time of the callAfter-hours and weekend visits are priced differently from a daytime appointment, and we say so first.

Many homeowner policies cover sudden water damage but not the failed part itself. If a leak soaked a floor or ceiling, ask your insurer before repairs begin and keep your photos and the invoice.

Toilets in Helena homes

Most Helena houses outside Old Town went up after 1990, when the city grew from 3,918 residents to 20,914 in 2020. That means a lot of builder-grade toilets and fill valves now at the age where flappers harden and supply lines weep.
